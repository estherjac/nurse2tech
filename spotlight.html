In their own words: how they made the switch

I get to use my clinical judgment in a completely different way.

IN HER WORDS

I worked as a pediatric intensive care (PICU) nurse for seven years. I loved the clinical environment, the critical thinking, and the constant learning that came with caring for complex patients. But after several years, I felt ready for a new challenge and wanted to explore other options.

My path into health tech wasn’t so straightforward.

I decided to pursue a master’s degree in Biomedical Sciences, focusing on endocrinology. During my studies, I discovered that I really enjoyed research and became increasingly interested in working with larger datasets and learning how to analyze them using computational tools.

After my master’s, I worked in a neuroscience research lab, where I learned on the job how to analyze large datasets. I found this work fascinating, but I was still unsure what I wanted my long-term career to look like.

Then a friend sent me a job opening at MedINT, a medical AI startup. As I read the description, I realized that this was an opportunity to bring together several parts of my background that I had previously thought of as separate: clinical experience, scientific research, and data analysis.

What had felt like a somewhat scattered career path suddenly made sense.

Today, I’m part of the clinical team at MedINT. Our work involves defining clinical requirements for the product, evaluating and validating its outputs, and assessing the clinical accuracy of the information it generates.

It’s an interesting intersection of medicine, research, technology, and product development. I get to use my clinical judgment in a completely different way, helping ensure that the technology we’re developing is clinically meaningful and reliable.

I also continue working part-time as a home hospice nurse, which allows me to maintain a direct connection to patient care. For me, it’s really the best of both worlds.

One of my biggest surprises was discovering how much I enjoy working in a startup environment. I always thought I was someone who needed to be on my feet, moving around and interacting with patients, and I couldn’t really imagine myself enjoying an office job.

But I’ve found the dynamic environment of a startup really stimulating. There’s always something new to learn, problems to solve, and opportunities to contribute. The work challenges me in ways I hadn’t anticipated.

HER ADVICE

Don’t undervalue your nursing experience if you’re looking to transition into tech. The clinical knowledge, critical thinking, and practical experience nurses bring are very valuable in the health tech world.

IN HER WORDS

I started my nursing career in the emergency department at Tel Aviv Sourasky Medical Center (Ichilov). Two years in the ER taught me to think fast, prioritize under pressure, and notice when something small was about to become big.

I then moved to the U.S., earned my American nursing license, and worked as a cardiac telemetry nurse at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston.

My first step away from the bedside was almost unplanned. I applied for a care coordinator role at an ACO and got it. I later moved into business development, where I saw how value-based care, data, and incentives shape patient care.

From there, I decided to expand my business skills and earned my MBA at the University of Michigan, followed by five years in leadership roles at Amazon.

The hardest part of the journey was learning to translate my nursing experience into business language. Interviewers often carry an image of what a nurse is: empathetic, caring, great soft skills. All true - but that’s only part of the picture. Nurses also triage competing priorities, make high-stakes decisions with incomplete information, and keep a whole system running on a bad night. What helped was a simple shift: when I told stories from the ER or the cardiac unit, I started calling my patients "customers." Same people, same care, just a word the interviewer already understood. Suddenly they could see what I'd actually done: understood a customer's needs under pressure, solved problems in real time, and delivered results. Once I learned to tell my story in their language, the doors opened.

I also realized something else: nurses are incredibly adaptable. At Amazon, I moved from vendor management in retail to product management in health to program management in operations. Each move meant new jargon, stakeholders, and a steep learning curve.

Looking back, I realized I'd been training for this my whole career. Nurses do it constantly. We float between departments, switch care settings, move between hospitals, even between healthcare systems. Every move means learning new protocols and teams, fast. Reinventing ourselves is part of the job. I just hadn't realized how valuable that skill is outside the hospital.

After 11 years in the U.S., I moved back to Israel. Today, I’m a digital health lead at Tzafon Medical Center, leading projects to connect rehabilitation devices to the EMR and to build a data hub that gives researchers secure access hospital data.

HER ADVICE

My advice to nurses considering a move into tech?

Learn the language of business and data, because your clinical judgment only lands if you can translate it.

And don’t discount your nursing experience. It’s your edge, not your gap.

I didn’t leave nursing behind. I found a different way to take care of patients - by fixing the systems around them.

TamaraPostpartum nurse → Clinical Data Analyst, Droxi AI

IN HER WORDS

I studied nursing at Northeastern University in Boston and eventually made Aliyah to Israel. After two grueling years of transferring my U.S. nursing license, I began working as a mother-baby nurse.

I’ve always been incredibly proud to be a nurse. But bedside nursing exposed me to larger systemic challenges in healthcare. I became curious not only about caring for the patient in front of me, but about how we could solve larger problems in healthcare.

When I decided to explore health tech, I completed a six-month data analytics course. It didn’t give me all the hard skills I use today, but it introduced me to a new world.

My first health tech role was as a junior data annotator at a company using NLP models to analyze conversations from seniors’ homes and identify clinical changes. Within a few months, I was developing annotation guidelines and translating clinical logic into rules for the model. More importantly, I was surrounded by amazing people and mentors who made me excited to come to work every day.

I asked a lot of questions. I asked data scientists how the models worked and tried to understand not just what I was doing, but why. Their willingness to teach gave me the space to be curious and shaped where I am today.

Today, I’m a Clinical Data Analyst at Droxi AI, where we use AI to reduce administrative burden on physicians and give doctors back time that too often spills into their evenings as “pajama time.”

I didn’t get here because I had a traditional technical background. I got here by combining clinical knowledge with curiosity, analytical thinking, and a willingness to tackle ambiguous problems. At times, moving into tech even felt selfish - my work-life balance improved while my nursing friends continue doing incredibly demanding work in the hospital.

The hardest part of leaving the bedside was identity. Being a nurse is a huge part of how I see myself. But the biggest surprise has been realizing that I didn’t stop being a nurse when I left the bedside. Clinical judgment, critical thinking, curiosity, and comfort with ambiguity are skills I rely on every day.

HER ADVICE

My advice to nurses looking to make the transition: be willing to take a calculated risk, and ask questions. Lots of them.

My first health tech role was junior and paid less, but it got me through the door. I trusted myself to learn once I got there and found people who never made me feel stupid for asking questions.

A couple of years later, I’m still learning every day, and I couldn’t be happier with where that curiosity has taken me.

Moving away from the bedside didn’t mean leaving nursing behind. It meant learning how to use my nursing brain in a completely different way.

#1

Getting your foot in the door

“Preparation meets opportunity.”

Nurses are involved in almost every part of healthcare. So why don’t we hear more about them in health tech and innovation?

It’s something I’ve been thinking about while building Nurse2Tech, especially here in Israel, where we have such a strong health tech ecosystem.

I’ve been curious about the different paths nurses take into this space, so I’ve started speaking with people who’ve made that move themselves.

I recently had a great conversation with Sergey Vasilenko MHA MBA, who started his career as a nurse and has since worked across clinical care, data, health tech, startups, and hospital innovation.

We talked about his journey and what it actually takes for nurses to make that transition.

One thing we discussed was that nurses bring so many valuable skills, but those skills don’t always translate directly into the tech world.

You might be great at prioritizing patients, assessing urgency, and making quick decisions, but how do you show a product team that those skills could be valuable in product management?

Part of the transition is learning how to communicate your clinical experience in a way that’s relevant to the role you want, while also building new skills.

Interested in product? Take a course, learn the terminology, understand how product teams work, or try a small project. It won’t make you a product manager overnight, but it can help bridge that gap.

A big theme of our conversation was getting your foot in the door.

Your first health tech role doesn’t have to be perfect. That first opportunity is often the hardest, but once you’re in, you can build experience, make connections, and figure out your next step.

We also talked a lot about networking. Don’t wait until you need a job. Speak to people in product, AI, data, QA, and innovation. Ask how they got there. Go to events. Get involved. There are so many roles in health tech that many of us were never exposed to as nurses.

As Sergey put it, “Preparation meets opportunity.”

And looking at where his career has taken him, I’d say that approach has worked out pretty well 😄

Thanks, Sergey, for a great conversation and for sharing your perspective! 💙
